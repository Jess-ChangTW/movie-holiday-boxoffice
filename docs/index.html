Do holidays sell movie tickets?

Studios pile horror films into the weeks before Halloween and romance into the week before Valentine's Day. Does it pay off? I combined 2,636 US theatrical releases with box office data to find out, and to separate the holiday effect from the fact that studios save their biggest films for these dates.

What I found

Scheduling first, then box office, then a test of whether the advantage survives controls.

  1. Studios clearly bet on the holidays

    Horror's share of weekly releases jumps from a 20% average to 30-34% in the five weeks before Halloween, then falls to the year's lowest right after. Romance clusters in the week before Valentine's Day: a smaller effect, but significant (17.5% vs 13.3%, p = 0.001).

  2. The advantage is relative: everything else slumps

    October and early February are weak weeks for most films. In the Halloween window, other films' median gross halves while horror rises. The matched genre bucks a seasonal decline rather than riding a general boom.

  3. About half is selection, half is the holiday

    Studios schedule bigger-budget and franchise films into these windows. After controlling for budget, franchise, MPA rating, IMDb rating, year and month, a premium of about 50-60% remains for both horror (p ≈ 0.01) and romance (p ≈ 0.04).

  4. Raw numbers can mislead

    Horror released 2-3 weeks before Halloween looks like the sweet spot in raw medians ($53.9M). With controls, the gap disappears: studios simply place their biggest horror titles there. The one robust timing pattern is that opening after Halloween hurts.

How I built it

A 13-step Python pipeline, each step a script with an auto-generated report.

Collect56,393 US theatrical titles from TMDB; US gross for 4,761 films from OMDb
CleanRemoved placeholder dates and re-releases; strict genres tagged by both TMDB and IMDb
DescribeGenre x week heatmap, timing distributions, z-tests on release shares
CompareBootstrap CIs on medians, difference-in-differences on log gross
ControlOLS with genre x window interaction, robust SEs, five nested models
PresentTableau story with a parameter that switches holidays

Data quality decisions

  • Used box office, not TMDB vote counts, to define a real US release. This fixed a false decline in film counts after 2014.
  • Flagged re-releases (Studio Ghibli re-runs, Hamilton) where TMDB and OMDb dates differed by over a year.
  • Used medians and log gross: 8.8% of films earn 62% of all box office.
  • Skipped Box Office Mojo because its robots.txt disallows automated access.

Limitations

  • Observational data: controls reduce but do not remove studio selection bias.
  • Small holiday groups (35-40 matched films per window), so intervals are wide.
  • IMDb rating is measured after release and may over-control.
  • OMDb gross is not inflation-adjusted and has small errors for some titles.
